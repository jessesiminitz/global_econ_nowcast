The data. Up to 22 monthly indicators — business surveys, trade and factory output, an oil-price gauge, financial-market and credit conditions, tech/chip demand, housing, jobless claims, retail sales, and confidence surveys — pulled primarily from Haver Analytics, with FRED, yfinance, OECD, IMF, and the World Bank as fallbacks when a source is unavailable (py/01_build_panel_real.py). A single broken source drops that one indicator rather than blocking the update — this run pulled 20 of 22 live; the rest were temporarily unreachable (no Haver subscription configured, and the free FRED endpoint was rate-limited) and were skipped rather than filled with stale numbers.

The models. Two statistical approaches compete to predict GDP growth from that panel: a factor model that condenses the whole panel into a few underlying trends, and an elastic-net regression that picks a small, stable set of indicators directly. 04_backtest.py tests both against history and 05_export.py publishes whichever wins — currently the elastic-net model (see the comparison above). 7 of the 20 indicators available this cycle are currently meaningful drivers of the estimate, shown below with their fitted weights.

Memorandum

Global Growth Nowcast

Re: World real GDP, Q2 2026Date: August 19, 2026

World GDP growth in Q2 2026 is tracking at an annualized +4.0 percent, modestly above the global economy’s 3.6 percent long-run trend, and an acceleration from the 3.0 percent pace booked in Q1 2026. The improvement is concentrated in stronger consumer spending and a resilient labor market, only partly offset by softer industrial production. This is a nowcast — a real-time estimate built from monthly data — not the official GDP figure, which will not be confirmed for several more months.

Why We Nowcast

Governments report GDP quarterly, and the final figure for a given quarter is typically not confirmed until well after that quarter has ended; for a global, PPP-weighted aggregate like this one, confirmation lags further still, since it depends on every major economy having reported. By the time the official number arrives, it is too stale to inform a near-term decision. A nowcast closes that gap by tracking the economy in real time, using monthly data that arrives faster than GDP itself — business surveys, trade flows, factory output, jobless claims, retail sales, and financial-market indicators. None of these series is GDP, but together they move closely with it and are available weeks to months sooner.

How We Build the Estimate

We combine roughly 20 monthly indicators — spanning trade, industrial activity, the labor market, consumer spending, and financial conditions — into a single statistical estimate of GDP growth. Two different modeling approaches are tested side by side against more than a decade of history, including the 2008 financial crisis and the 2020 pandemic, and we use whichever has actually predicted GDP more accurately, quarter by quarter, rather than committing in advance to one method. That comparison is re-run every time the estimate is refreshed, so the model in use can change — and has changed, in past refreshes — as conditions change.

The Data

Data is drawn primarily from Haver Analytics, a professional economic-data platform, with the Federal Reserve, the OECD, the IMF, and other public sources as fallbacks when a series is unavailable. We favor data that is genuinely monthly, so it updates faster than GDP; available with only a short lag; and has a long, consistent history, so it can be tested against past recessions and recoveries rather than only recent, calmer periods. Two series — Credit spreads and Leverage / credit-to-GDP — are sitting out this update because their available history is either too short or has stopped updating; both are re-tested every cycle and will return automatically once the data resumes.

What the Results Are Telling Us

The headline number — +4.0 percent for Q2 2026 — is a read on the current quarter, not a distant forecast: it sits just one quarter beyond Q1 2026, the last quarter with a confirmed GDP print, so it is built on real monthly data that has already come in for Q2 2026, not a long-range guess. The chart on the Dashboard tab also carries the estimate several quarters further out, to show where the model expects growth to head next, but those later quarters are extrapolated rather than grounded in incoming data and should be weighted less heavily than this one.

Even this quarter’s number is an estimate, not a confirmed figure: historically, this model’s GDP estimates have missed the actual print by about 3 percentage points on average, so the exact decimal matters less than the direction and rough magnitude it points to — growth running above trend and accelerating from last quarter, not the reverse.

Key Drivers of Growth

Consumer spending, measured through retail sales, is the single largest positive contributor this quarter — households are spending more, not less. The labor market is the next-largest positive contributor: jobless claims are falling, a sign employers are not yet cutting workers despite the slowdown elsewhere. That slowdown shows up directly: industrial production is the largest single drag this quarter, even as the positive drivers outweigh it. Smaller movements in bank lending conditions, emerging-market borrowing costs, and bank equities round out the picture but are not currently decisive.

Bottom Line

The economy is growing faster than its long-run trend, carried by the consumer and the labor market even as manufacturing cools. We will continue to update this estimate monthly as new data arrives and will flag if the balance of drivers shifts.
