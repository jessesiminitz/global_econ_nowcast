The data. Up to 22 monthly indicators — business surveys, trade and factory output, an oil-price gauge, financial-market and credit conditions, tech/chip demand, housing, jobless claims, retail sales, and confidence surveys — pulled primarily from Haver Analytics, with FRED, yfinance, OECD, IMF, and the World Bank as fallbacks when a source is unavailable (py/01_build_panel_real.py). A single broken source drops that one indicator rather than blocking the update — this run pulled 15 of 22 live; six US-only proxies were deliberately set aside to keep this a global-only panel, and one more (credit spreads) was dropped automatically for insufficient history — see the note below the indicator table for the full list.

The models. Two statistical approaches compete to predict GDP growth from that panel: a factor model that condenses the whole panel into a few underlying trends, and an elastic-net regression that picks a small, stable set of indicators directly. 04_backtest.py tests both against history and 05_export.py publishes whichever wins — currently the elastic-net model (see the comparison above). 14 of the 15 indicators available this cycle are currently meaningful drivers of the estimate, shown below with their fitted weights.

Memorandum

Global Growth Nowcast

Re: World real GDP, Q2 2026Date: August 20, 2026

World GDP growth in Q2 2026 is tracking at an annualized +3.3 percent, modestly below the global economy’s 3.6 percent long-run trend but an acceleration from the 3.0 percent pace booked in Q1 2026. This update runs the nowcast on a global-only indicator panel: six US-specific proxies — bank lending standards, home prices, credit-to-GDP leverage, jobless claims, bank equities, and financial conditions — have been set aside so the estimate rests only on indicators with genuine multi-country coverage. That change lowers the estimate by roughly 0.6 percentage point relative to the equivalent full-panel reading (+4.0 percent) but does not change the model ranking: the elastic-net model remains the more accurate of the two candidate specifications, particularly in stress periods. This is a nowcast — a real-time estimate built from monthly data — not the official GDP figure, which will not be confirmed for several more months.

Why We Nowcast

Governments report GDP quarterly, and the final figure for a given quarter is typically not confirmed until well after that quarter has ended; for a global, PPP-weighted aggregate like this one, confirmation lags further still, since it depends on every major economy having reported. By the time the official number arrives, it is too stale to inform a near-term decision. A nowcast closes that gap by tracking the economy in real time, using monthly data that arrives faster than GDP itself — business surveys, trade flows, factory output, jobless claims, retail sales, and financial-market indicators. None of these series is GDP, but together they move closely with it and are available weeks to months sooner.

How We Build the Estimate

We combine roughly 15 monthly indicators — spanning trade, industrial activity, the labor market, consumer spending, and financial conditions — into a single statistical estimate of GDP growth. Six additional indicators with genuine information value but only US-level coverage (bank lending standards, home prices, credit leverage, jobless claims, bank equities, and financial conditions) are set aside for this run to keep the panel global. Two different modeling approaches are tested side by side against more than a decade of history, including the 2008 financial crisis and the 2020 pandemic, and we use whichever has actually predicted GDP more accurately, quarter by quarter, rather than committing in advance to one method. That comparison is re-run every time the estimate is refreshed, so the model in use can change — and has changed, in past refreshes — as conditions change.

The Data

Data is drawn primarily from Haver Analytics, a professional economic-data platform, with the Federal Reserve, the OECD, the IMF, and other public sources as fallbacks when a series is unavailable. We favor data that is genuinely monthly, so it updates faster than GDP; available with only a short lag; and has a long, consistent history, so it can be tested against past recessions and recoveries rather than only recent, calmer periods. Six indicators are sitting out this update by design, not because the data is unavailable — they are US-only proxies with no clean global equivalent (bank lending standards, home prices, credit leverage, jobless claims, bank equities, and financial conditions) and can be reinstated at any time. A seventh, credit spreads, is absent because its available history only goes back to 2012, too short to date.

What the Results Are Telling Us

The headline number — +3.3 percent for Q2 2026 — is a read on the current quarter, not a distant forecast: it sits just one quarter beyond Q1 2026, the last quarter with a confirmed GDP print, so it is built on real monthly data that has already come in for Q2 2026, not a long-range guess. The chart on the Dashboard tab also carries the estimate several quarters further out, to show where the model expects growth to head next, but those later quarters are extrapolated rather than grounded in incoming data and should be weighted less heavily than this one.

Even this quarter’s number is an estimate, not a confirmed figure: historically, this model’s GDP estimates have missed the actual print by about 2.9 percentage points on average, so the exact decimal matters less than the direction and rough magnitude it points to — growth running modestly below trend but accelerating from last quarter, not the reverse. The gap with the equivalent full-panel reading (+4.0 percent) is broadly consistent with the six set-aside indicators having been modest net positive contributors, combined, in the prior run; the elastic-net model was also refit on the smaller panel, so the two figures are not a clean like-for-like decomposition.

Key Drivers of Growth

Consumer spending, measured through retail sales, is again the single largest contributor this quarter, adding roughly 0.6 percentage point. Business sentiment, captured by the composite PMI, is the next-largest positive driver. Those gains are largely offset by industrial production — the largest single drag this quarter — and by softer global equity performance; together the two subtract close to 0.9 percentage point. Smaller movements in COVID-era stringency effects, oil prices, and the remaining financial-market indicators round out the picture but are not currently decisive.

Bottom Line

Growth is running modestly below trend but accelerating from Q1, carried by the consumer and by business sentiment even as factory output and equity markets soften. Restricting the panel to genuinely global indicators did not change that picture materially: the estimate and the model ranking are consistent with the full-panel run. We will continue to update this estimate monthly as new data arrives and will flag if the balance of drivers shifts, or if reinstating the US-only proxies would meaningfully change the read.
